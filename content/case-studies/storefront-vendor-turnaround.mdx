## The problem

I led a first-of-its-kind project for the company: a Shopify storefront for our team's printed marketing materials. Nobody had done this before, so the brief I started from was vague. I ran with what the team thought they wanted and hired a vendor to build and run it on Shopify, while I managed the project.

The result was not good. Leadership reviewed it and called it a failure, directly.

## What I did

I treated the feedback as the clearest brief I had gotten so far. The first attempt had shown exactly what we did not want, which meant I could finally define what we did.

- **Diagnosed the failure.** I pinned down why the first vendor fell short so the next search had real criteria, not guesses.
- **Ran a structured vendor search.** I interviewed several companies and requested samples, pricing, and deadlines.
- **Tested how they worked, not just what they made.** I asked how each vendor handled problems and communication, since service had been a big part of the first failure.
- **Made the call.** I ended the first vendor relationship and hired the new one.
- **Managed delivery.** The new vendor handled the Shopify build. I owned requirements, timelines, feedback rounds, and the internal launch.

## The result

The relaunched storefront was significantly better in both quality and service. It was announced company-wide, and the same leadership that called the first version a failure was thrilled with the second.

## What I took from it

**A vague brief produces a vague result.** The first vendor was not the only problem. Starting without clear requirements was.

**A failure can be the best requirements document you get.** Honest criticism told me exactly what to look for.

**Owning a project includes owning its failure.** I did not hand the problem back. I fixed it and delivered.
